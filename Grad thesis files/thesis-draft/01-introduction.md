# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 Motivation

Modern cardiology produces an enormous volume of electrocardiogram (ECG) recordings, and deep neural networks have become remarkably effective at reading them: detecting arrhythmias, localizing ischemic events, and flagging conduction abnormalities at a level that, on narrow tasks, approaches that of expert cardiologists [25]. The clinical appeal is direct. Cardiac arrhythmias such as atrial and ventricular ectopy, and chronic conditions such as myocardial infarction and conduction disorders, are common, sometimes life-threatening, and frequently under-detected in routine care. An automated screen that triages ECGs at scale — in primary clinics, in wearable monitors, in emergency departments — could shorten the path from signal to diagnosis. Achieving that level of accuracy, however, depends on training data, and clinical ECG data is among the most tightly governed data that exists.

Patient records are protected by regulation in essentially every jurisdiction — the United States' HIPAA, the European Union's GDPR, and Türkiye's KVKK among them — and by professional and ethical obligation independent of any statute. The straightforward engineering recipe for a strong ECG classifier, namely pooling recordings from many hospitals into one central training set, is precisely the operation that these protections are designed to prevent. A single institution rarely holds enough labeled data, across enough patient subpopulations and acquisition devices, to train a model that generalizes; yet the institutions that collectively do hold such data cannot legally or ethically merge it. This is the structural tension that motivates privacy-preserving distributed learning for medical artificial intelligence.

**Federated learning (FL)** [2], [3] is the canonical response. Instead of moving data to a central trainer, FL moves the training to the data: each participating site trains a model locally on its own records and shares only model updates — gradients or parameters — with a coordinating server, which aggregates them into a global model and broadcasts it back. Over many such rounds the global model improves as if it had seen every site's data, while no raw patient recording ever leaves its institution. The standard aggregation rule, Federated Averaging (FedAvg) [2], simply averages the client updates weighted by their dataset sizes.

Federated learning removes the most obvious privacy exposure — raw data movement — but it does not by itself make training private. The shared model updates are functions of the local data, and a body of work has shown that they can leak it: membership-inference and gradient-inversion attacks can recover whether a particular record was in the training set, or even reconstruct individual training inputs, from the updates a client transmits [4], [31]. For data as sensitive as a patient's ECG, "we only shared the gradients" is not a privacy guarantee. This motivates layering **differential privacy (DP)** [5] on top of federated learning.

Differential privacy gives a formal, quantitative guarantee that the output of a computation is statistically insensitive to the presence or absence of any single record. The dominant mechanism for training deep networks under DP is **DP-SGD** [5], which clips each per-sample gradient to a bounded norm and adds calibrated Gaussian noise before the optimizer step, yielding a tunable $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP guarantee whose strength is controlled by a privacy budget $\varepsilon$. DP-SGD composes cleanly with federated learning: each client simply runs DP-SGD on its local optimizer, and because every transmitted update is already DP-protected at its source, the server's aggregation needs no further privacy machinery [4]. In principle, then, private federated ECG classification is just "FedAvg with DP-SGD locally."

In practice, one architectural component breaks this clean composition: **batch normalization (BN)** [6]. The difficulty is mechanical and is developed in full in Chapter 2; in brief, BN normalizes each sample using the mean and variance of the whole mini-batch, which couples the samples together and makes the notion of a *per-sample* gradient — the object DP-SGD must clip — ill-defined. Production DP libraries enforce this: Opacus [7], the standard PyTorch implementation of DP-SGD, refuses at setup time to wrap any model that contains a `BatchNorm` module. Its recommended remedy is to replace every BN layer with GroupNorm [8], which computes per-sample channel-group statistics and is therefore privacy-safe.

That remedy is where the tension this thesis addresses becomes sharp. Batch normalization is not only a convenience in the federated setting — it is the substrate of one of the most effective remedies for *non-IID* data, which is the defining difficulty of realistic federated learning. Client datasets in medicine are almost never identically distributed: a cardiology referral center, a general hospital, and a single-condition clinic see systematically different mixes of normal and abnormal beats. Under such heterogeneity, FedAvg drifts and under-converges. **FedBN** [9] is a simple and influential fix: it averages the convolutional and linear parameters across clients exactly as FedAvg does, but it *excludes* the BatchNorm parameters and running statistics from aggregation, so that each client's BN layers stay local and act as a learned, per-client adapter to that client's own data distribution. FedBN's entire mechanism is the BatchNorm state that the GroupNorm fix deletes. Replacing BN with GroupNorm to satisfy Opacus therefore does not merely change a normalization layer — it removes the personalization mechanism that made the federated model robust to heterogeneity in the first place.

## 1.2 Problem statement

The space of reasonable options under this three-way tension between BatchNorm, DP-SGD, and federated learning is small, and each corner sacrifices something. One can keep FedBN and abandon DP, buying personalization at the cost of any formal privacy guarantee. One can keep DP and abandon FedBN, applying DP-FedAvg with GroupNorm and accepting the loss of the BN-based personalization mechanism. Or one can attempt to *compose* the two by applying DP-SGD selectively — only to the parameters that actually leave the client. This third option is what the source paper, and this thesis, call **DP-FedBN**.

The idea is straightforward in principle and subtle in practice. Under the FedBN protocol the BatchNorm parameters and running statistics never cross the federation boundary: they are never aggregated and never broadcast. In the standard honest-but-curious federated threat model, a parameter that is never transmitted cannot be inferred from transmissions, and so it requires no DP protection at all. The only parameters that need DP are the non-BN (convolutional and linear) parameters that *do* cross the boundary. The principled construction, therefore, applies DP-SGD to the non-BN parameters while leaving the BN state to update locally and freely. Implementing this on top of off-the-shelf Opacus, however, is not obvious: it requires a non-trivial freeze-then-unfreeze maneuver to make Opacus's validator accept a BN-containing model, two parallel optimizers — one DP-protected, one plain — and careful coordination of the parameter naming that Opacus introduces when it wraps the model. The central methodological problem of this thesis is to make that composition correct, library-compatible, and properly privacy-accounted, and then to ask the empirical question its title poses: **when does federated BatchNorm actually help under differential privacy?**

## 1.3 Contributions

This thesis makes three contributions, two empirical and one methodological. The framing is deliberately measured, and one point of prior art must be stated up front. The term "DP-FedBN" is not introduced here: it already appears in **ADCOL** (Li, He, and Song, ICML 2023) [1], whose Appendix B.11 and Table 17 include a one-paragraph "DP-FedBN" baseline implemented by clipping and adding **Laplace** noise to communicated, aggregated model updates — that is, the *party-level* (client-level) DP recipe of Geyer et al. [4] — evaluated on five digit-recognition datasets. The contribution of this work is therefore neither the DP-FedBN concept nor a new aggregation rule nor a new privacy mechanism. To our knowledge, this is the **first record-level DP-SGD composition of FedBN's BatchNorm-locality with explicit treatment of the Opacus engineering obstacle**, together with the first systematic medical empirical characterization of that composition across heterogeneity regimes and privacy budgets. Concretely:

1. **Methodological — a record-level DP-SGD construction of DP-FedBN, with engineering documentation.** We give a dual-optimizer construction, compatible with off-the-shelf Opacus 1.5, that applies DP-SGD per-sample gradient clipping to the non-BN parameters while keeping the BN parameters and running statistics local. Unlike the party-level Laplace baseline of ADCOL [1], this construction provides **record-level** $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP — strictly stronger than a party-level Laplace mechanism — and we document the implementation obstacles (the `ModuleValidator` rejection of `BatchNorm`, the freeze-then-unfreeze workaround whose discussion lives only in an Opacus GitHub issue from 2022 [10], the state-dict prefix mismatch under `GradSampleModule`, and the incompatibility of the ExpandedWeights backend with `BatchMemoryManager`), and validate that the construction passes Opacus's `ModuleValidator` without sacrificing FedBN's mechanism.

2. **Empirical 1 — the eval-regime flip is task-agnostic.** On two datasets with substantially different tasks — MIT-BIH five-class beat-level classification and PTB-XL binary record-level classification — and at matched non-IID partitions, FedBN ranks *worst* under pooled-central evaluation while ranking *best* under per-client local evaluation. The central-versus-local macro-F1 gap reaches **+0.72** on MIT-BIH label-skew ($C{=}2$). We argue that the choice between pooled-central and per-client local evaluation matters more than the choice of aggregation method in many realistic deployment scenarios.

3. **Empirical 2 — DP-FedBN's local advantage is partition-dependent.** Under DP, the comparison between DP-FedBN and DP-FedAvg+GroupNorm is partition-sensitive. DP-FedBN preserves a **+0.17** local-F1 edge on MIT-BIH label-skew at $\varepsilon{=}3$, but *loses* under Dirichlet $\alpha{=}0.1$ on both datasets (−0.008 on MIT-BIH and −0.234 on PTB-XL at $\varepsilon{=}3$). We give a mechanistic explanation — DP-driven activation noise propagates to the per-client BN parameters without the cross-client averaging that smooths it for shared parameters — and translate the findings into a heterogeneity-typed decision rubric for medical FL practitioners.

A fourth observation supports the first contribution: naive DP-FedAvg on a BatchNorm-containing model is *empirically refused* by Opacus's `ModuleValidator` at every finite $\varepsilon$, which is the concrete motivation for a non-naive composition rather than a side remark.

## 1.4 Organization of the thesis

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows. **Chapter 2 (Fundamental Concepts)** develops, with the equations needed later, the background on which everything rests: ECG signals and arrhythmia classification, deep learning for ECG, federated learning and the non-IID problem, batch normalization and why it motivates FedBN, differential privacy and DP-SGD with its privacy accounting, and the precise mechanical conflict between batch normalization and DP-SGD. **Chapter 3 (Related Work)** situates DP-FedBN against prior art in privacy-preserving federated learning, differential privacy in healthcare machine learning, batch normalization in federated learning, and deep learning for ECG, and positions the contribution honestly against ADCOL [1]. **Chapter 4 (Methodology)** presents the DP-FedBN construction in full: the system and threat model, the model architecture, the record-level DP-SGD dual-optimizer construction, the handling of BatchNorm statistics under DP and FL, the privacy accounting, and a complete algorithm box. **Chapter 5 (Experimental Setup)** details the datasets and preprocessing, the non-IID partitioning scheme, the model and training configuration, the differential-privacy parameters, the evaluation regimes, and the implementation. **Chapter 6 (Results and Discussion)** reports the centralized baselines, the non-IID federated benchmark, the eval-regime flip, the differential-privacy privacy–utility frontier, and the cross-task replication, and interprets the partition-dependent DP behavior, the practitioner rubric, and the limitations. **Chapter 7 (Conclusion and Future Work)** summarizes the contributions, restates the limitations, and lays out concrete directions for extending the work.
