# 3. Related Work

This chapter situates DP-FedBN within four strands of prior work — federated aggregation under non-IID data, differential privacy in (healthcare) federated learning, batch normalization in federated learning, and deep learning for ECG — and then positions the contribution precisely against the one prior paper that already uses the name "DP-FedBN." The goal is an honest account of what is established, what is borrowed, and what is genuinely new here, in the measured terms the contribution warrants.

## 3.1 Federated aggregation under non-IID data

Federated averaging (FedAvg) [2] is the foundation on which essentially all of this literature builds: clients run local SGD and the server averages their updates weighted by sample count (Chapter 2). FedAvg is communication-efficient and works well when client data is identically distributed, but it degrades under the statistical heterogeneity that defines realistic federated deployments, and a large body of work addresses that degradation. Kairouz et al. [16] survey the field comprehensively and catalogue non-IID data as one of its central open problems.

The responses divide broadly into methods that change the *optimization* and methods that change the *model* or its *aggregation*. **FedProx** [11] is the canonical optimization-side response: it adds a proximal term to each client's local objective that anchors the local solution to the broadcast global model, damping client drift at the cost of some local fit. The strength of the proximal term is a hyperparameter, and a poorly chosen value can make FedProx under-perform plain FedAvg — a sensitivity that this thesis observes directly. On the aggregation side, **FedBN** [9] leaves FedAvg's averaging rule unchanged for convolutional and linear parameters but excludes batch-normalization parameters and running statistics from the average, so that each client's BN layers calibrate to its own data distribution and act as a learned per-client domain adapter. FedBN was originally validated on feature-shifted image partitions (the DomainNet and Office–Caltech benchmarks), where keeping BN local improved over FedAvg.

A broader family of **personalized federated learning** methods shares FedBN's goal of producing models adapted to each client rather than a single global compromise. Several build directly on FedBN's normalization idea: extensions include batch-specific batch normalization combined with sharpness-aware minimization for privacy-preserving brain–computer interfaces [12], and personalized data transformations learned per client [13]. This thesis treats plain FedBN as its personalization baseline and does not deploy these extensions, both to keep the comparison clean and because the questions it asks — about evaluation regime and about composition with differential privacy — are orthogonal to which specific personalization rule is used. A simple performance-weighted aggregation baseline, here called FedPerf, is also included to contextualize FedBN; it is not claimed as a contribution and is described in Chapter 5.

## 3.2 Differential privacy in federated and healthcare machine learning

Differential privacy entered deep learning with **DP-SGD** [5], which clips per-sample gradients to a bounded norm and adds calibrated Gaussian noise (Chapter 2). Its composition with federated learning is direct: each client runs DP-SGD on its local optimizer, and because every transmitted update is already differentially private at its source, the server's aggregation requires no further privacy mechanism [4]. The **Opacus** library [7] provides the standard PyTorch implementation, including a `PrivacyEngine`, an RDP-based privacy accountant, the `ModuleValidator` architectural-compatibility checker, and memory-management utilities for per-sample gradient computation; this work uses Opacus 1.5 throughout.

A crucial axis within DP for federated learning is the **granularity** of the guarantee, introduced in Section 2.5.1. **Record-level** DP, the natural product of DP-SGD with per-sample clipping, protects each individual training record. **Client-level (party-level)** DP, due to Geyer et al. [4] and McMahan et al. [32], instead clips and noises the entire aggregated client update, protecting whether a whole institution participated. The two protect different units and are calibrated by different mechanisms; both are legitimate, and the appropriate choice depends on whether the privacy concern is the individual patient record or the participating site. This distinction is the hinge on which the present contribution turns, and Section 3.5 returns to it.

In healthcare specifically, federated learning has been advocated as the natural paradigm for multi-institutional medical AI precisely because it avoids centralizing protected patient data [3]. Layering differential privacy on top is widely regarded as necessary rather than optional in this setting, given that shared updates can leak training data (Section 2.5). Within medical federated learning under DP and non-IID conditions, the most directly comparable benchmark is **FedOnco-Bench** [15], which provides a careful, reproducible evaluation of privacy-aware federated tumor segmentation under heterogeneity. Notably, FedOnco-Bench evaluates FedBN and DP-SGD as *competing alternatives* — one or the other — rather than as a single combined method, which is exactly the composition this thesis constructs and characterizes. The orthogonal question this thesis surfaces — whether one should report pooled-central or per-client local performance — is, we believe, of independent interest to this medical-FL benchmarking community.

## 3.3 Batch normalization in federated learning and existing DP workarounds

The incompatibility between batch normalization and DP-SGD is mechanical and unavoidable (Section 2.6): BN couples samples within a batch through shared statistics, which makes the per-sample gradient that DP-SGD must clip ill-defined, and Opacus's `ModuleValidator` consequently rejects any model containing a `BatchNorm` module. The standard workaround, `ModuleValidator.fix()`, replaces every BN layer with **GroupNorm** [8], whose per-sample channel-group statistics are DP-safe. This is sound for differential privacy and is the conventional way to train normalization-heavy architectures under DP-SGD.

The difficulty, for the present setting, is that this workaround is incompatible with FedBN's design. GroupNorm carries no learnable running statistics, so there is no per-client BN state for FedBN's exclusion rule to keep local — the very mechanism that gives FedBN its non-IID advantage is gone. Two recent works address the broader BN-under-DP gap differently. **ResNetFed** [14] modifies the network architecture globally to support differentially private federation for privacy-preserving pneumonia detection from chest radiographs, at the cost of replacing batch normalization throughout the network. **FedOnco-Bench** [15], as noted above, benchmarks FedBN and DP-SGD side by side as alternatives rather than composing them. Neither keeps BatchNorm alive *and* obtains a record-level DP-SGD guarantee at the same time; the construction in Chapter 4 does, by exploiting the fact that under FedBN the BatchNorm state never crosses the federation boundary and therefore never needs DP protection.

It is worth being explicit about why the obvious alternatives are unsatisfying for a deployment that genuinely needs both personalization and privacy. Abandoning DP keeps FedBN's advantage but forfeits any formal privacy guarantee. Abandoning FedBN — using DP-FedAvg with the GroupNorm fix — keeps the privacy guarantee but discards the personalization mechanism. Replacing BN with GroupNorm globally, as ResNetFed does, changes the architecture and again removes the per-client normalization state. The remaining option, and the one this thesis pursues, is to compose the two by protecting only what crosses the federation boundary.

## 3.4 Deep learning for ECG and arrhythmia classification

The application domain has its own mature literature. The **MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database** [18] is the long-standing reference benchmark for beat-level classification, and the **ANSI/AAMI EC57** standard [19] defines the five-class beat grouping and the inter-patient evaluation conventions that make results comparable across studies; classical and modern approaches alike — from morphology-and-interval feature engineering [20] to ensembles fusing temporal and morphological information [21] — are calibrated against it. Deep learning has pushed beat- and rhythm-classification performance substantially: convolutional networks trained on large single-lead datasets have reached cardiologist-level performance on rhythm classification [25], and transferable convolutional representations have become a standard recipe for MIT-BIH beat classification [26].

For record-level classification, the **PTB-XL** dataset [22] is the largest publicly available clinical 12-lead ECG corpus, with standardized diagnostic super-class labels and canonical stratified folds that fix the train/test split across studies. Strodthoff et al. [27] provide the reference deep-learning benchmark on PTB-XL, characterizing how convolutional and recurrent architectures perform across its diagnostic tasks. These ECG benchmarks are, however, almost entirely *centralized*: they assume the data is pooled. The contribution of this thesis is not a new ECG architecture or a new benchmark score — the model used here is a deliberately standard BN-heavy 1-D CNN — but a study of how federation, personalization, and differential privacy interact on these two well-understood tasks.

## 3.5 Positioning: DP-FedBN and ADCOL (ICML 2023)

The name "DP-FedBN" is not introduced by the source paper or by this thesis. It already appears in **ADCOL** — *Adversarial Collaborative Learning on non-IID features* (Li, He, and Song, ICML 2023) [1] — whose Appendix B.11 and Table 17 include a one-paragraph "DP-FedBN" baseline. That baseline is implemented by clipping and adding **Laplace** noise to communicated, aggregated model updates — that is, the **party-level (client-level)** DP recipe of Geyer et al. [4] — and is evaluated on five digit-recognition datasets at privacy budgets $\varepsilon \in \{2, 5, 10\}$. Stating this clearly is part of calibrating the present claim honestly: DP-FedBN is a prior name, and the dual-optimizer composition idea is not claimed here as a conceptual invention.

The contribution of this work is therefore deliberately narrow and specific. To our knowledge, this is the **first record-level DP-SGD composition of FedBN's BatchNorm-locality with explicit treatment of the Opacus engineering obstacle**, together with the first systematic medical empirical characterization of that composition across heterogeneity regimes and privacy budgets. Three concrete differences distinguish it from the ADCOL baseline:

1. **Privacy granularity.** This work applies DP-SGD via *per-sample gradient clipping*, yielding a **record-level** $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP guarantee, which is a stronger guarantee than the party-level Laplace mechanism of the ADCOL baseline in the comparison drawn by the source paper. The two operate at different units of adjacency (Section 2.5.1).

2. **Engineering.** This work documents the concrete obstacle that makes the composition non-trivial in practice — Opacus's `ModuleValidator` rejection of `BatchNorm` — and packages a freeze-then-unfreeze workaround whose enabling discussion exists only in an Opacus GitHub issue from 2022 [10], along with the further pitfalls (state-dict key prefixing under `GradSampleModule`, and the incompatibility of the ExpandedWeights backend with the batch-memory manager) detailed in Chapter 4. The ADCOL baseline, being a party-level Laplace mechanism on aggregated updates, never encounters this per-sample machinery and so never confronts the obstacle.

3. **Empirical scope.** This work characterizes the composition on **two medical ECG tasks, across six non-IID partition regimes and three privacy budgets** — about forty configurations — rather than a single label-balanced digit-recognition setting.

In summary, this thesis contributes neither a new aggregation algorithm nor a new privacy mechanism. It contributes a record-level DP-SGD *construction* of the FedBN-plus-DP composition with documented Opacus engineering, a multi-partition, multi-budget medical evaluation of that construction on MIT-BIH and PTB-XL, and — orthogonally — the observation that the choice between pooled-central and per-client local evaluation is itself a decision that can reverse which method appears best. The following chapters develop the construction (Chapter 4), the evaluation (Chapters 5–6), and that orthogonal observation in full.
